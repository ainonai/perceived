## Work, purpose, and constraints

**Congruence** means understanding the hand you were dealt and arranging life around it. Recognizing a predisposition helps you decide when to resist it, when to change the conditions around it, and when a different setting would let it become a strength. Imitating an incompatible temperament can make all three decisions harder. ### Congruence and environmental fit

Knowing a predisposition gives you a place to intervene. In Plomin’s snack example, removing tempting food from the house changes the decision before the late-night craving arrives. Watching an explanatory video can also change later behavior. The interest that brought you to it and the knowledge you take from it both enter the process. Use actual strengths when choosing work. A skilled trade may fit better than university pursued for status, while an intense or socially atypical temperament may find an outlet in specialized work. Appearance and presentation also affect which roles feel convincing. Environmental fit includes what you can do and how others read you doing it. ### Persistence and learning

Failure can leave useful “scar tissue.” Conditioning, practice, and experience remain even if a fight is lost or canceled. A failed project can likewise teach details that carry into the next one. The final result matters, but it is not the only return from the work. ### Incentives, restraint, and influence

Appeal to what the other person stands to gain. At work, the continuing value of an unfinished project can provide a stronger reason to keep someone than gratitude for past service alone. The incentive need not be narrowly financial: generosity and a sense of being needed can also give a person a reason to act. ### Cooperation, conflict, and unequal starting conditions

**Tit-for-tat** begins with cooperation, continues when cooperation is returned, and responds to cheating. Repeated exchanges give conduct a consequence: the other person’s last move changes the next response. The same pattern connects everyday reciprocity with the island thought experiment about cooperative and exploitative environments. **Mogging** means overshadowing someone in appearance or stature. **Mogguilt** is discomfort about doing so, especially beside another man’s partner. Julius describes looking away, reducing his apparent height, and being particularly considerate toward someone he perceives as having a worse starting hand. ### Pleasure, purpose, and continuity

**Generational replication** extends biological continuity beyond having one child. Children must survive and eventually reproduce; their descendants face the same task. Resources, partners, and the surrounding community matter because reproduction does not end with a single successful birth. ### Technology, valuation, and timing

Recognizing a mispricing and surviving until the market recognizes it are different problems. Irrationality can outlast solvency. A correct short position can lose money while enthusiasm for the company continues, so being right about the eventual outcome does not settle the timing. [V188 · 04:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LVCbDfrVsPw&t=261s) [V188 · 05:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LVCbDfrVsPw&t=329s)

**Alpha**, in investing, is an informational advantage the market has not fully priced. Holding it can mean months or years of apparently poor performance and requires the capital to endure that interval. **Incomplete revelation** explains how an advantage can remain available: a useful number is public, but buried deep in a filing that few people have examined closely.
