# Success, happiness, and living under constraints

## 1. Congruence is more than resignation

Congruence means arranging one’s life around dispositions, strengths, limits, and values rather than copying an incompatible ideal. The advice is not simply to obey every impulse. His “when to fight your biology” discussion describes a spectrum: some predispositions are worth resisting, some can be managed by changing environment, and some are so central or costly to fight that a compatible life makes more sense. He uses weight, anxiety, attention, ambition, and career fit as examples. Severity, feasibility, and what the person values all matter. Julius’s determinism removes ultimate self-authorship without removing the ordinary causal effects of action. Knowledge can redirect effort; a video can affect a trajectory; a different environment can make a trait useful rather than disabling. ## 3. Work, perseverance, and learning from failure

His fight-camp account is unusually concrete. Months of preparation, physical strain, doubt, and injury taught him that experience leaves useful “scar tissue” even when a project fails. The lesson is not merely “keep going”; it is to analyze performance and genuinely improve rather than silently quit while maintaining a routine. ## 12. Happiness as signal, purpose as direction

Julius distinguishes pleasure from fulfillment. Drugs, compulsive rewards, and directly chasing happiness supposedly “scam biology,” borrowing joy against later costs. Sustained fulfillment comes from living congruently, doing one’s duties, finding useful work, and contributing to reproduction or a community that supports descendants. Wealth is subject to adaptation; expecting happiness every day makes ordinary suffering feel like failure. He recommends low outcome expectations and noticing spontaneous joy as a gift. ## 14. Investing: truth, timing, and staying solvent

Julius’s investment principle is that recognizing a useful technology is different from paying a sensible price for exposure to it. Being early can be costly because an insight does not pay until others recognize it. He recommends diversification and regular investing, while sometimes claiming dollar-cost averaging always beats timing—even perfect timing.
