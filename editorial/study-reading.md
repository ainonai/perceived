# How to read a study skeptically

Julius points to a gap in a plot of published studies. Small studies usually give a wider spread of results, yet one side of that spread looks thin. His explanation is the **file drawer**. When studies with disappointing results stay unpublished, the studies that reach print give readers a different picture. He introduces the problem through Stuart Ritchie’s *Science Fictions*. ### What reached the journals

In the longer video, Julius follows an example where researchers could see what the journals left out. Erick Turner’s team matched articles to FDA reviews of 74 antidepressant trials. The FDA judged 38 positive. Of the 51 trials that reached journals, 48 appeared positive. Some unfavorable trials went unpublished and others were written up more favorably. That is **publication bias** in a form a reader can see. The two percentages describe trials, with a different denominator on each side. ### Before the paper reaches print

Bias can start inside a single study. Imagine measuring mood, sleep, appetite, and a dozen other outcomes. Julius likens testing them all and reporting only the winner to buying a stack of lottery tickets and showing people the one that paid out. **P-hacking** exploits choices like which outcomes to test or which participants to include. To judge the result, you need to know how many chances the analysis gave itself and how it accounted for them. [V181 · 01:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cWhbzFZDWYE&t=66s) [V182 · 20:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u7lsEOFUwQA&t=1241s) [Simmons et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611417632)

Now imagine the depression result falls short but patients report sleeping better. Julius pictures the researchers rewriting the introduction to make sleep look like their prediction all along. He compares it to the Texas sharpshooter painting a bull’s-eye after firing at the barn. That is **HARKing**. The distinction in Norbert Kerr’s paper is how the finding is presented. An unexpected result can suggest what to test next. Writing it up as an original prediction gives the reader the wrong account of how it was found. [V181 · 01:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cWhbzFZDWYE&t=99s) [V182 · 22:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u7lsEOFUwQA&t=1338s) [Kerr, 1998](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327957pspr0203_4)

The same example shows what **outcome switching** looks like. A trial sets depression scores at twelve weeks as its main measure. The article foregrounds sleep or anxiety and lets the original result fade into the background. The practical check is to read the paper beside its protocol or registry entry and see what happened to the outcome the trial set out to measure.
