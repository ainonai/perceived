# How to read a study skeptically

Start with three questions. What did the researchers plan to measure? How did they decide which results to report? Which completed studies reached print? Julius draws on Stuart Ritchie’s *Science Fictions* to follow those decisions from the first analysis to the published record. [V181 · 00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cWhbzFZDWYE&t=0s) [V182 · 24:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u7lsEOFUwQA&t=1449s)

**P-hacking** means searching through analysis choices until one produces a statistically significant result, then presenting that result without showing the search. Try the full sample, just the men, just the women, then switch to another outcome. Each extra test gives chance another opportunity to produce a result that looks striking. Ask which tests were planned, which were tried afterward, and how the analysis accounted for testing several possibilities. [V181 · 01:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cWhbzFZDWYE&t=66s) [V182 · 20:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u7lsEOFUwQA&t=1241s) [Simmons et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22006061/)

**HARKing** means presenting a hypothesis formed after seeing the results as if it were the original prediction. Julius imagines a depression trial that misses its main outcome but finds that patients slept a little better. The researchers rewrite the introduction to make sleep look like the target all along. That is the Texas sharpshooter painting a bull’s-eye around the holes after firing. The sleep result can generate a new hypothesis to test in a fresh study. **Publication bias** changes which studies you get to read. When favorable findings are more likely to reach journals, they take up more of the visible record. Turner’s team compared journal articles with FDA reviews of 74 antidepressant trials. The FDA judged 38 of the 74 trials positive. In the published literature, 48 of the 51 published trials appeared positive. The comparison tracks how trials were classified or presented. Put the paper beside its protocol or registry entry. Julius uses a trial that sets depression scores at twelve weeks as its main outcome, then promotes sleep or anxiety when the depression result falls short. That is **outcome switching** when the paper leaves the change unexplained. Check what happened to the original outcome, how large the reported effect was, and whether other studies find it too.
