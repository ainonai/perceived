# How to read a study skeptically

There’s a gap in the published record. Plot the results of small studies and they usually fan out more widely than the results of large ones. When one side of that spread looks unusually empty, some of the missing studies may be sitting in a **file drawer**. A disappointing result stays there while a striking result travels from journal to headline. By the time you read the literature, part of the evidence has already disappeared. That’s one of the problems Stuart Ritchie follows in *Science Fictions*. Erick Turner’s team could see what the journals left out. They had FDA reviews of 74 antidepressant trials and matched them to the papers that reached print. The FDA judged 38 positive. In the journals, 48 of the 51 published trials appeared positive. Some unfavorable trials vanished from the published record. Others survived with a more favorable presentation. The same body of work looks dramatically different depending on which record you read. That’s **publication bias**. The same selection can happen inside a single study. Buy a stack of lottery tickets and show people only the one that paid out. The win looks improbable because nobody sees how many chances you gave yourself. **P-hacking** works through choices about which outcomes to test, which participants to include, or when to stop collecting data. Each extra chance matters when the paper shows you only the result that cleared the bar. Imagine a trial comes up short on depression, but the patients sleep a little better. The researchers rewrite the introduction so better sleep looks like the prediction all along. It’s the Texas sharpshooter painting the bull’s-eye after firing at the barn. That’s **HARKing**, or hypothesizing after the results are known and presenting the hypothesis as if it came first. Better sleep is an interesting result. Give it a fresh test and find out whether it holds up. [V181 · 01:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cWhbzFZDWYE&t=99s) [V182 · 22:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u7lsEOFUwQA&t=1338s) [Kerr, 1998](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327957pspr0203_4)

To spot **outcome switching**, put the paper next to the trial’s original protocol. If the protocol promises depression scores at twelve weeks and the paper leads with sleep, look for what happened to those scores. The headline tells you which result made it to the front of the paper. The protocol gives you the question the trial was designed to answer.
