# How to read a study skeptically

The antidepressant trials looked overwhelmingly positive in the journals. In the FDA’s files, only about half were. When Erick Turner and his colleagues matched the agency’s reviews to the published papers, they found that some unfavorable trials had never reached print. Others had been written up in ways that made their results look better. [V182 · 12:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u7lsEOFUwQA&t=751s) [V182 · 13:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u7lsEOFUwQA&t=812s) [Turner et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMsa065779)

That gap illustrates **publication bias**. When favorable results are more likely to reach print, the papers you find give you a rosier picture than the full set of trials. Stuart Ritchie takes up the problem in *Science Fictions*. One clue is how small studies scatter when you plot their results. Small samples tend to produce a wider spread of estimates. If there are plenty of small studies with large effects and few with weak ones, ask which results might still be sitting in a **file drawer**. Selection can start inside a single study. A winning lottery ticket looks remarkable until you see the stack of losing tickets beside it. **P-hacking** works the same way. Researchers can try different outcomes, change which participants count, or keep collecting data until a result crosses the threshold for statistical significance. If the paper reports only the analysis that worked, the result looks more convincing than it would if you saw all the attempts. Suppose a trial finds little change in depression scores, but the patients sleep a little better. The researchers rewrite the introduction so better sleep looks like the result they predicted all along. That’s **HARKing**, short for hypothesizing after the results are known. Think of the Texas sharpshooter painting a bull’s-eye around the bullet holes after firing at a barn. Better sleep is still worth investigating. A fresh trial gives the researchers a chance to test that prediction. [V181 · 01:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cWhbzFZDWYE&t=99s) [V182 · 22:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u7lsEOFUwQA&t=1338s) [Kerr, 1998](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327957pspr0203_4)

In that same trial, the protocol might name depression scores at twelve weeks as the main outcome. If the paper quietly gives that role to sleep instead, that’s **outcome switching**. Read the paper beside the original protocol. Find the result the researchers set out to measure, then see how it compares with the one they chose to emphasize.
